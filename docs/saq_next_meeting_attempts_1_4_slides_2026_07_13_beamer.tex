\section{Four Ideas We Tested --- and Why We
Stopped}\label{four-ideas-we-tested--and-why-we-stopped}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Four Ideas We Tested --- and Why We Stopped}
Date: 2026-07-13

Revised: 2026-07-18

Status: \textbf{current draft; not yet presented}

This deck is for a research-direction discussion. It does not present a
new method. Detailed evidence remains in the source branches and in
\texttt{docs/saq\_research\_direction\_registry.md}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{1. What I Want To Settle Today}
\protect\phantomsection\label{1-what-i-want-to-settle-today}
Over the last round, we tested four plausible ways to improve SAQ. Each
idea had a reasonable motivation. None produced a result strong enough
to become the next paper direction.

I would like us to agree on two things:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  which parts are worth keeping as evidence or baselines; and
\item
  what an idea must show before we open another implementation branch.
\end{itemize}

The useful outcome of this work is a narrower search space, not a hidden
win that needs one more parameter sweep.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{2. A Short Reminder: What SAQ Is Doing}
\protect\phantomsection\label{2-a-short-reminder-what-saq-is-doing}
SAQ stores a compressed version of each database vector. That makes it
cheaper to compare a query with many candidates, but the compressed
distance is only an estimate of the true distance.

Three different questions therefore matter:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Does the compressed vector resemble the original vector?}
\item
  \textbf{Does the estimated distance rank the right candidates near the
  cutoff?}
\item
  \textbf{Does the whole search system remain fast and affordable?}
\end{itemize}

Several attempts improved the first question without improving the other
two. That distinction explains most of the decisions in this deck.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{3. The Short Answer}
\protect\phantomsection\label{3-the-short-answer}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Attempt 1 --- change or shorten the PCA representation:} the
  encouraging result did not survive replication, and the lossy version
  failed even under a deliberately favorable test.
\item
  \textbf{Attempt 2 --- train scalar codebooks exactly:} useful as a
  stronger offline baseline, but better reconstruction did not reliably
  improve search. The inner exact optimizer is also established prior
  work.
\item
  \textbf{Attempt 3 --- judge results by distance quality as well as
  Recall:} this changed one GIST interpretation, but it changed the
  measurement, not the method, and did not replicate as a positive
  result on DEEP.
\item
  \textbf{Attempt 4 --- allow non-power-of-two cardinalities:}
  mathematically possible, but the registered exact pipeline exceeded
  its cost ceiling. A later recovery path never produced a valid
  scientific result and is now closed.
\end{itemize}

My recommendation is to retain the lessons and close all four method
lines.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{4. Attempt 1: Can We Represent The Vector Better?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{4-attempt-1-can-we-represent-the-vector-better}
Principal component analysis, or \textbf{PCA}, rotates the vector
coordinates so that large sources of variation are easier to identify.

We tested two versions of the same broad idea:

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  keep all dimensions, but learn the rotation from residual vectors
  rather than raw vectors; and
\item
  keep only 576 of 960 dimensions, then store the norm of the discarded
  tail as a compact summary.
\end{enumerate}

The first version preserves exact geometry before compression. The
second throws information away, so we gave it an unusually favorable
early test: an exact retained part and an exact tail norm, before adding
quantization error.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{5. Attempt 1: What The Evidence Says}
\protect\phantomsection\label{5-attempt-1-what-the-evidence-says}
On GIST, residual PCA reduced distance-estimation error by about
\textbf{0.60\%} at one accurate stage and \textbf{0.33\%} with the full
code. The fast stage became worse, and the ranking improvement was not
reliable.

We then repeated the frozen comparison on CIFAR. The small GIST effect
did not repeat: only 4 of 10 seeds favored residual PCA at the accurate
stage, the no-rotation control pointed the wrong way, and the fast-stage
degradation was supported by the data.

The reduced-dimension version also failed early. Even with exact
retained coordinates and exact tail norms, its top-100 agreement was
\textbf{0.99289}, below native SAQ at \textbf{0.99462}. The missing tail
inner product---not numerical precision---caused the remaining error.

So we stopped before building a projected index. This does not prove
that PCA is universally optimal. It says these two replacements did not
earn further systems work.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{6. Attempt 2: Does Exact Training Help Search?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{6-attempt-2-does-exact-training-help-search}
A scalar codebook replaces many numeric values with a small set of
representatives. Standard Lloyd training is iterative and may settle at
a local solution. Exact one-dimensional dynamic programming can find the
best partition for the chosen histogram.

That sounds attractive, but it leaves two separate questions:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Does exact histogram training reduce reconstruction error?
\item
  If it does, are the nearest-neighbor rankings actually better?
\end{itemize}

The first question is about fitting compressed values. The second is the
one the search system ultimately cares about.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{7. Attempt 2: A Better Fit Was Not A Reliable Search Win}
\protect\phantomsection\label{7-attempt-2-a-better-fit-was-not-a-reliable-search-win}
There were genuine positive cases. On the audio dataset, one setting
reduced mean squared reconstruction error by about \textbf{15.5\%} and
raised Recall at 100 from \textbf{0.95275} to \textbf{0.95675}.

But the relationship did not hold consistently:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  increasing histogram resolution kept improving reconstruction while
  Recall stopped improving monotonically;
\item
  on DEEP at 4 bits, one setting cut raw reconstruction error by about
  20\% but made Recall at 100 worse; and
\item
  across CIFAR and DEEP, the sign and size of the Recall change depended
  on the bit budget and allocation objective.
\end{itemize}

There is also a novelty limit. Exact one-dimensional scalar clustering
was already developed by Wu (1991) and later accelerated by Grønlund et
al. (2017); the outer bit allocation is a classical discrete allocation
problem. We keep this implementation as a demanding offline baseline,
not as our method.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{8. Attempt 3: Were We Using The Wrong Quality Measure?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{8-attempt-3-were-we-using-the-wrong-quality-measure}
Recall at 100 asks whether we returned the same item identifiers as the
exact top 100. A distance-quality score asks a softer question: how much
farther are the returned items than the exact ones?

This matters near a crowded boundary. Replacing one almost-equally-close
item with another hurts Recall, even when the geometric loss is tiny.

We therefore replayed two frozen SAQ plans and reported both measures.
Nothing about the encoder, index, or search procedure changed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{9. Attempt 3: One Decision Changed, But No Method Emerged}
\protect\phantomsection\label{9-attempt-3-one-decision-changed-but-no-method-emerged}
At the historical GIST reference point, the alternative plan could not
reach the default plan\textquotesingle s Recall target. Under the
distance-quality measure, however, a directly measured point was
slightly better in quality and \textbf{1.078 times} faster.

That is worth reporting, but it is not uniform dominance:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{39.6\%} of paired GIST queries became worse;
\item
  the positive conclusion did not carry to the two DEEP controls; and
\item
  the distance-quality measure comes from prior work.
\end{itemize}

The correct conclusion is narrow: one previous rejection was sensitive
to the metric. Future evaluations should show both Recall and geometric
quality. We did not discover a new encoding or search mechanism.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{10. Attempt 4: Can We Use A Fixed Bit Budget More
Flexibly?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{10-attempt-4-can-we-use-a-fixed-bit-budget-more-flexibly}
Consider two factors that must share a word with at most 16 joint
states.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Restricting each factor to a power of two permits \textbf{4 by 4},
  using all 16 states.
\item
  Allowing arbitrary positive integers also permits \textbf{3 by 5},
  using 15 states.
\end{itemize}

For uneven data, the second shape can fit better while using the same
stored word. A synthetic example confirms that this possibility is real.

But a larger set of mathematical choices is not yet a database method.
We still have to construct the codebooks at acceptable cost and then
show better search quality at comparable speed and memory.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{11. Attempt 4: The Cost Gate Ended The Study}
\protect\phantomsection\label{11-attempt-4-the-cost-gate-ended-the-study}
The registered synthetic implementation passed its correctness checks.
The next question was deliberately mundane: can we afford the exact
construction and evidence pipeline before reading benchmark data?

The projected cost was \textbf{24.17025 CPU-hours}, just above the
frozen \textbf{24-hour} ceiling. According to the rule fixed in advance,
that was a stop. It is a pipeline-cost result, not evidence that
arbitrary cardinalities help or hurt search.

A later attempt to rebuild a cleaner execution path never reached a
valid scientific outcome. Its final invocation recorded that it started,
but did not produce a trustworthy completion record. We therefore
classify the runtime as unknown and have stopped further artifact
recovery.

There is no natural-data result, no search result, and no performance
claim for this direction.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{12. What These Four Attempts Have In Common}
\protect\phantomsection\label{12-what-these-four-attempts-have-in-common}
Each attempt improved or clarified something local:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  a slightly better distance estimate on one dataset;
\item
  a lower reconstruction objective;
\item
  a more informative evaluation metric; or
\item
  a larger mathematical feasible set.
\end{itemize}

The local improvement did not survive the next question: replication,
ranking, novelty, or cost.

This is the main lesson I would carry forward. We should test the
shortest link between an attractive local objective and the final search
claim before we build the surrounding machinery.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{13. What Is Still Useful}
\protect\phantomsection\label{13-what-is-still-useful}
The work was not wasted. We now have:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  SAQ as a well-understood strong baseline;
\item
  exact scalar training as a tougher offline comparison;
\item
  a reason to report both exact-identifier Recall and distance quality;
\item
  examples showing why reconstruction error alone is a poor search
  proxy; and
\item
  a practical precedent for stopping expensive construction before
  touching benchmark queries.
\end{itemize}

These belong in the evaluation design of the next project. They do not
need to be promoted into contributions of their own.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{14. My Recommendation}
\protect\phantomsection\label{14-my-recommendation}
Close the four method lines and select the next problem from primary
work, before writing more implementation code.

For a new direction, I suggest four entry conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  the limitation appears on at least two strong, independent baselines;
\item
  the closest published work leaves a specific gap that is not filled by
  a direct combination of known techniques;
\item
  one cheap, frozen test can reject the idea early; and
\item
  construction, memory, and query cost are part of the acceptance rule
  from the beginning.
\end{enumerate}

The decision I need from this meeting is whether we agree with that
closure and selection rule.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A1. Terms Used In The Talk}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a1-terms-used-in-the-talk}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{SAQ:} the compressed-vector baseline studied in this project.
\item
  \textbf{PCA:} principal component analysis, a rotation that orders
  directions by variance.
\item
  \textbf{Residual:} the difference between a vector and its assigned
  cluster center.
\item
  \textbf{Recall at 100:} the fraction of exact top-100 neighbor
  identifiers that appear in the returned top 100.
\item
  \textbf{Distance quality, written as 1/Ratio:} how close the returned
  distances are to the exact top-100 distances; closer to 1 is better.
\item
  \textbf{QPS:} queries per second; higher is faster.
\item
  \textbf{CPU-hour:} one processor core working for one hour.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{A2. Attempt 1 Evidence Boundary}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a2-attempt-1-evidence-boundary}
\textbf{Full-dimensional replacement}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  GIST residual-PCA change: accurate-stage error \texttt{-0.602\%},
  full-code error \texttt{-0.331\%}, but fast-stage error worsened.
\item
  CIFAR replication: fast \texttt{+0.0317\%} worse, accurate
  \texttt{+0.00463\%} worse, full \texttt{-0.0235\%}; confidence and
  seed requirements failed.
\item
  Decision: close the one-dataset estimator effect.
\end{itemize}

Source: \texttt{saq-transform-analysis@3d94840},
\texttt{docs/saq\_transform\_phase1b\_external\_replication\_evidence\_2026\_07\_10.md}.

\textbf{Lossy 960-to-576 projection}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Favorable projected oracle top-100 agreement: \texttt{0.992890625}.
\item
  Native full-dimensional SAQ: \texttt{0.994617188}.
\item
  Decision: fail the early ranking gate; projected index construction
  was not authorized.
\end{itemize}

Source: \texttt{saq-lossy-projection-analysis@051ec6a},
\texttt{docs/saq\_lossy\_projection\_lp0\_gate\_a\_evidence\_2026\_07\_11.md}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{A3. Attempt 2 Evidence Boundary}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a3-attempt-2-evidence-boundary}
\small

Representative results:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Audio, 4 bits: reconstruction error per value fell from
  \texttt{280,023} to \texttt{236,478}; Recall at 100 rose from
  \texttt{0.95275} to \texttt{0.95675}.
\item
  DEEP, 4-bit rank-boundary setting: raw reconstruction-error ratio was
  \texttt{0.8016}, while Recall at 100 changed by \texttt{-0.00215}.
\end{itemize}

The result supports two limited claims: exact-histogram training is a
useful offline baseline, and lower reconstruction error does not
guarantee better nearest-neighbor ranking.

It does not support a new scalar-quantization method claim. Exact
one-dimensional dynamic programming is prior work (Wu, 1991; Grønlund et
al., 2017), and the outer allocation is a classical discrete
rate-allocation problem. White and Singal (2026) is recent adjacent
quantization work that explicitly treats exact one-dimensional k-means
as an older baseline.

Sources: \texttt{vectordb@f51b487},
\texttt{reports/scalar\_training\_exact\_hist\_audit\_2026\_06\_30/README.md}
and \texttt{docs/saq\_limitation\_transfer\_memo\_2026\_07\_02.md};
prior-work boundary at \texttt{saq-caq-one-shell-repair@433e8ea}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{A4. Attempt 3 Evidence Boundary}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a4-attempt-3-evidence-boundary}
Frozen GIST reference:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  default plan at 200 probes: distance quality \texttt{0.999985301}, QPS
  \texttt{9264.9};
\item
  alternative plan at 280 probes: distance quality \texttt{0.999987869},
  QPS \texttt{9986.9};
\item
  measured speed ratio: \texttt{1.07793}.
\end{itemize}

Query-paired result: 39.6\% worse, 23.0\% equal, and 37.4\% better. Both
DEEP controls remained below their default distance-quality targets.

Decision: keep this as metric-sensitivity evidence. Do not claim
per-query dominance, cross-dataset replication, or a new mechanism.

Source: \texttt{saq-ratio-metric-analysis@146dc16},
\texttt{docs/saq\_attempt3\_a3\_2\_a3\_3\_decision\_2026\_07\_13.md}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{A5. Attempt 4 Evidence Boundary}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a5-attempt-4-evidence-boundary}
The synthetic 16-state example showed a real feasible-set difference:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  arbitrary \texttt{(3,5)} cardinalities: zero distortion;
\item
  power-of-two \texttt{(4,4)} cardinalities: \texttt{0.1} distortion per
  vector.
\end{itemize}

This was instrument validation only. No benchmark data or search
evaluation was involved.

The exact construction pipeline then projected \texttt{24.170246892361}
CPU-hours against a frozen \texttt{24.0} CPU-hour ceiling. The
registered decision was \texttt{NO\_GO\_EXACT\_SOLVER\_COST}.

The later V2 execution path produced no valid terminal record. The
audited project decision is to stop further artifact recovery; the
runtime remains unknown rather than being reclassified as a scientific
failure.

Sources: \texttt{saq-arbitrary-cardinality-analysis@f1b464b} and
\texttt{saq-arbitrary-cardinality-feasibility-v2@3577edd}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A6. What We Can And Cannot Say}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a6-what-we-can-and-cannot-say}
Supported:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the tested PCA replacement did not replicate;
\item
  the registered norm-only projection failed its favorable early ranking
  test;
\item
  exact scalar training improved offline fit more reliably than search;
\item
  one GIST comparison changed under a distance-based quality measure;
  and
\item
  Attempt 4 stopped on registered pipeline cost, followed by project
  closure without a scientific V2 result.
\end{itemize}

Not supported:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  PCA is universally optimal;
\item
  exact scalar training never helps search;
\item
  distance quality makes the alternative SAQ plan generally better;
\item
  arbitrary cardinalities cannot help on real data; or
\item
  any of these attempts produced a new method or moved the quality-speed
  frontier against the strongest baseline.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
